Step 2

Player Priors

A 2026 Week 1 projection uses no 2026 usage. For each player, we calculate recency-weighted rates from 2021–25, then shrink noisy player rates toward the position average. Team volume comes from the player’s 2026 destination team, using one distinct row per team game from 2025.

Recency weighting

game weight = 0.60 ^ (2026 − season)
raw player rate = weighted mean across all player-game rows

The decay is applied to each game, so a season's total weight is games × season decay. For three full seasons, the latest supplies 51.0% of the weight. McCaffrey's missed 2024 time makes his 2025 weight mass 57.2%; 2021 supplies 3.1%.

Destination-team volume

expected pass = mean(SF pass attempts across 17 games in 2025) = 33.76
expected rush = mean(SF rush attempts across 17 games in 2025) = 28.29

These are means, not fixed future values. Step 4 draws a different pass/rush total in every simulation.

Feature construction
history = games[games.season < 2026]

# Player rate is pulled toward a positional rate
shrunken = (games_played * player_rate + strength * position_rate)
           / (games_played + strength)

Week 1 matchup adjustment

Each player is joined to the known Week 1 schedule. We calculate what the opponent previously allowed to the player's position, pull that noisy number toward league average, and limit its effect to ±8%. This makes matchup a visible refinement—not the reason a projection is high or low.

matchup multiplier = clip(shrunk opponent points allowed / league points allowed, .92, 1.08)
final simulated points = component PPR points × matchup multiplier

The defense rate is built from completed seasons only, using the same .60 recency decay as player priors plus 12 league-average pseudo-games. A missing opponent uses a neutral 1.00 multiplier. The full methodology documents the formula and fields.

Step 3

Role Uncertainty

Role uncertainty changes the distribution of targets and carries. It is not a fantasy-point adjustment. A player with uncertain workload is allowed to draw a wider share of the team opportunity pool.

Implemented role-uncertainty rule
sample_penalty = max(0, 1 - min(games, 32) / 32)
instability     = min(target_share_sd / .25, 1)

uncertainty = .08 + .32*sample_penalty + .22*team_change
              + .22*rookie + .12*instability

share_concentration = 70 * (1 - uncertainty) + 5
Real 2026 examples

Jonah ColemanRBDEN010.71624.9

Lower concentration means a wider beta-binomial share distribution. Coleman is not assigned fewer points because he is a rookie; his future target/carry role is modeled as less certain.

Step 4

Event Simulation

Each player is simulated 20,000 times. Every run draws team volume, opportunities, efficiency, and touchdowns, then converts the resulting stat line to full-PPR points.

Simulation sequence
pass_attempts = Poisson(Gamma(mean=33.76, CV=.18))
rush_attempts = Poisson(Gamma(mean=28.29, CV=.20))

targetable_passes = Binomial(pass_attempts, .82) # team_targets / pass_attempts proxy
targets           = BetaBinomial(targetable_passes, target_share, concentration)
carries           = BetaBinomial(rush_attempts, rush_share, concentration)
receptions        = BetaBinomial(targets, catch_rate, catch_concentration)
yards             = opportunities * Gamma(efficiency_mean, efficiency_CV)
touchdowns        = Poisson(opportunities * shrunken_TD_rate)
PPR               = receptions + .1 * yards + 6 * touchdowns
What “targetable-pass rate” means: it is a team-level bridge between quarterback pass attempts and recorded targets. For each historical player-game row, the code computes team_targets / team_pass_attempts, averages it within position, and bounds the result between .45 and .82. It is not McCaffrey’s target share, catch rate, or a claim that 82% of his own routes are targetable. Here it turns roughly 34 team pass attempts into a pool of roughly 28 recorded targets, and McCaffrey’s .217 share allocates about 6.0 of them to him. The .82 ceiling is a guardrail for this simplified proxy; a play-level target model would be a natural next improvement.

Step 5

Model Evaluation

We replay past Week 1s as if they had not happened yet. For the 2025 test, for example, the model sees only 2021–24 and is then compared with the real 2025 Week 1 result. Repeating that for 2022–25 tells us whether the point estimates and ranges are useful.

How to read the results
questionmetricwhat a good result looks like
How far off is the typical single-number forecast?median absolute errorLower is better. A value of 2.83 means the typical P50 miss is 2.83 fantasy points.
Does a stated 50% range behave like a 50% range?50% coverageAbout .50 of actual outcomes should land between P25 and P75.
Does a stated 80% range behave like an 80% range?80% coverageAbout .80 should land between P10 and P90. Lower means overconfidence; much higher usually means the range is too wide.
How wide is the range?interval widthSmaller is useful only when coverage is close to its target. A huge range can capture almost everything without helping a decision.
Are all five percentiles placed sensibly?pinball lossLower is better. This is the technical score used to check P10, P25, P50, P75, and P90 together.
Walk-forward calibration, in plain English: before publishing a new range, we look at how earlier ranges missed. If past P10–P90 ranges were too narrow, we widen the next one; if they were too wide, we can tighten it. For the 2025 backtest, the correction is learned only from 2022–24 forecast errors—not the 2025 result being graded. For the current 2026 output, it uses completed 2022–25 Week 1 errors. This is why it is “walk-forward”: the model only learns from the past.

In the raw component model, the advertised 80% range actually caught 76% of outcomes. That means it was a little too tight. Calibration widened it; the calibrated range caught 87%, so it is now cautious rather than perfectly calibrated.

The position-normal baseline is a useful check: on the same 921 players, its typical point-projection miss is 2.82 points versus 2.87 for the calibrated component model. The component model is still the main output because it shows where the projection comes from—team volume, role, efficiency, touchdowns, and the separately visible matchup adjustment—rather than only returning a final number.
